<ParagraphTitle id="2-1-2-data-transmission">Data transmission</ParagraphTitle>

<ParagraphContent id="2-1-2-data-transmission">
Data transmission may take place over a short distance, such as between a computer and a printer, or over a long distance, such as between computers on a global network. Three factors must be considered:

- the direction of transmission;
- the method of transmission, including how many bits can be sent at the same time;
- synchronisation, so that data arrives in the correct order.

These factors are usually defined by a communication protocol.

### Simplex, half-duplex and full-duplex

**Simplex** transmission sends data in one direction only, from sender to receiver. Sending data from a computer to a printer is an example.

**Half-duplex** transmission sends data in both directions, but not at the same time. A walkie-talkie is an example: both users can send and receive messages, but only one can transmit at a time.

**Full-duplex** transmission sends data in both directions at the same time. A broadband internet connection is an example.

### Serial and parallel transmission

**Serial transmission** sends one bit at a time over a single wire or channel. The bits are sent one after another as one stream. Serial transmission works well over long distances and keeps the bits synchronised, but it is slower than parallel transmission.

```text
Sender  ->  10011011  ->  Receiver
```

Serial transmission can be simplex, half-duplex or full-duplex. A USB connection between a computer and a printer is an example.

**Parallel transmission** sends several bits, usually one byte, at the same time over several wires or channels. Each wire carries one bit. It is faster than serial transmission and works well over short distances.

Over longer distances, parallel data can become skewed: bits may arrive out of order because the wires do not carry the signals identically. Computer internal circuits use parallel transmission because the distances are short and high speed is important.
